For years, my world was kitchens: recipes, ingredients, deadlines, and the art of pastry. Today I build web and mobile apps. This is how that happened, what was hard, and what I'd tell anyone thinking about doing the same.

## A different kind of ambition

Working as a pastry chef taught me far more than cooking. It taught me discipline, patience, creativity and consistency. In a professional kitchen every detail matters, deadlines are real, and mistakes are expensive.

But I kept getting curious about something else: how websites work, how apps talk to each other, how developers turn an idea into a product people actually use and how can i make my touch and build something useful and solve a problem. That curiosity didn't go away. It grew year after year and become  a decision.

## The questions I had to face

I didn't have a computer science degree or years in IT. So I asked myself the questions every career-changer asks:

- Am I too late to start programming?
- Can I compete with people who studied computer science or Tech in general?
- Is moving from hospitality to software development even realistic?

What I eventually understood: a different background doesn't stop you from building a different future. I wasn't throwing away what I learned in the kitchen. I was taking it somewhere new.

## Starting from zero

I started with the fundamentals: **HTML, CSS and JavaScript**. Everything felt unfamiliar. A single missing character could break a whole page, and a problem that looked simple could take hours.

Then I joined the **SIMPLON full-stack and mobile development bootcamp ** and earned my certificate. That's where things got real: **React** and **React Native** for interfaces, **Node.js, Express and PostgreSQL** for the backend, **JWT** for authentication, **Docker**, and **Git and GitHub** for everything.

The biggest lesson? Coding isn't about memorizing syntax. It's about breaking a problem into small pieces, testing, failing, and trying again.

## The hard parts

It wasn't all exciting. There were moments of frustration and self-doubt. Sometimes it feels like everyone else is moving faster. Sometimes you spend hours on something another developer explains in five minutes.

I had to accept that being a beginner is part of the process. And honestly, that part felt familiar: nobody becomes a great pastry chef overnight either. You practice, you get it wrong, you improve your technique, and you repeat. Software development works the same way.

## Where my two worlds met

Something I didn't plan: my food background keeps showing up in what I build.

- **Take-a-Chef**, a mobile platform connecting clients with private chefs, built with React Native, Node.js and PostgreSQL.
- **Moroccan Gastronomy**, a website about Moroccan recipes and food culture, built with Next.js and live on its own domain.
- **Softness**, an editorial restaurant website designed around one action: reserving a table.

I know these worlds from the inside, and that helps me build products that make sense for the people who use them.

## What the kitchen taught me about code

My previous career didn't teach me to write code, but it gave me the mindset:

- **Attention to detail.** In pastry, a few grams change everything. In code, one wrong condition breaks the app.
- **Staying calm under pressure.** A kitchen never runs without surprises. Neither does debugging production.
- **Consistency.** Great results come from showing up every day, not from one perfect day.
- **Creativity with constraints.** Technique plus imagination, in both jobs.

## If you're thinking about changing careers

You don't need everything figured out before you start. You don't need every language, the best laptop or a perfect academic background. You need a realistic plan, consistency, curiosity, and the willingness to keep going when it gets hard.

Your previous experience isn't an obstacle. It might become your biggest advantage. And don't confuse being a beginner with being incapable.

## What's next

My journey is still unfolding. No overnight success, no magic shortcut. Just someone who chose a different path and keeps building, one project at a time.

I started in the kitchen. Now I'm building my future through technology, and this is only the beginning.

**Sometimes the most important career decision isn't choosing what you'll do for the rest of your life. It's having the courage to start becoming someone new.**
